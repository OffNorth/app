1Stock market basics✓

Trading means buying or selling an asset to profit from a price move, with a maximum loss decided in advance. Before looking at a chart, you need to know what you are trading, with which product, and what it costs.

1.1 Stocks, indices, markets

  • Stock: a share of a company (Apple, Nvidia). Its price moves with earnings, news and supply/demand.
  • Index: a basket of stocks summed up in a single number. The NAS100 (Nasdaq-100) groups about 100 of the largest non-financial companies listed on the Nasdaq.
  • Investing vs trading: an investor holds for months or years; a trader holds for a few minutes (scalping), a few hours (day trading) or a few days (swing).
  • Long / short: long = you buy and win if it goes up. Short = you sell and win if it goes down.

1.2 Products for trading the NAS100

You cannot buy “the index” directly: you go through a product that tracks it.

ProductCommon symbolFor whomPoints to watch
Index CFDUS100, NAS100, USTECBeginners, small accountsLeverage, spread, overnight fees (swap), counterparty = the broker
Micro E-mini futureMNQSmall accounts that want a real regulated market$2 per point, quarterly expiries
E-mini futureNQLarger accounts, prop firms$20 per point: a 100-point move = $2,000
ETFQQQ, QQQMInvesting, swing trading without leverageNo leverage, US market hours only

Prop firms: companies that make you pass a paid challenge to trade their capital. They often use the NAS100 as a CFD or NQ/MNQ. Always read their rules (max daily loss, drawdown) before paying.

1.3 The price and what it costs you

  • Bid / Ask: bid = price at which you sell, ask = price at which you buy. The difference is the spread, your first cost.
  • Slippage: difference between the price you wanted and the price you got, common during news.
  • Commission: fixed fee per order (futures, some CFD accounts).
  • Swap / overnight fees: cost of keeping a CFD open overnight.

1.5 Leverage and margin

Leverage lets you control a large position with little money. Example: with 1:20 leverage, €1,000 of margin controls €20,000 of exposure. A 1% move against you = -€200, i.e. 20% of your margin. Leverage multiplies gains AND losses: it is your position size (module 6) that decides your risk, not the displayed leverage.

2Technical analysis✓

Technical analysis is for one thing only: spotting where the price is likely to react, so you can place an entry, a stop and a target. It does not predict the future, it gives probabilities.

2.1 Japanese candlesticks

Each candle summarises a period (1 minute, 1 hour, 1 day) with 4 prices: open, high, low, close (OHLC).

  • Body: between open and close. Green = close above the open, red = below.
  • Wicks: the extremes reached and then rejected. A long wick = a rejection.
  • Key candles: hammer / pin bar (rejection of a low), shooting star (rejection of a high), bullish/bearish engulfing (a candle that engulfs the previous one), doji (indecision).
  • Rule: a candle on its own means nothing. It only counts if it forms at an important level.

2.2 Trend and market structure

  • Uptrend: higher highs (HH) and higher lows (HL).
  • Downtrend: lower lows (LL) and lower highs (LH).
  • Range: the price oscillates between a ceiling and a floor.
  • Break of structure (BOS): the price breaks the last high (uptrend) or the last low (downtrend): the trend continues.
  • Change of character (CHoCH): in an uptrend, the price breaks the last HL: first reversal signal.

2.3 Support, resistance and key levels

  • Support: an area where buyers have already stepped in. Resistance: an area where sellers have stepped in.
  • Draw zones, not lines to the exact point: on the NAS100, a zone is often 15 to 40 points wide.
  • Broken support often becomes resistance (and vice versa): that is the retest.
  • Levels to mark every morning: previous day high/low (PDH/PDL), Asian and London session high/low, previous close, round numbers (multiples of 100 and 500 points).

2.4 Liquidity

Traders' stops pile up just above obvious highs and below obvious lows. The price often goes to take them (a “sweep” or “stop hunt”) before turning the other way. Practical consequence: do not place your stop exactly on an obvious high or low, put it a little beyond.

2.5 Useful indicators (and only those)

IndicatorWhat it showsPractical use
Moving averages EMA 20 / 50 / 200The direction and strength of the trendPrice above the EMA 200 = buying bias; EMA 20 = pullback zone in a strong trend
VWAPThe session's volume-weighted average priceIntraday reference: institutions buy below the VWAP, sell above
RSI (14)The speed of the move, from 0 to 100Divergences at a key level; above 70 or below 30 is NOT a signal on its own
ATR (14)Average volatility per candleSize your stop (e.g. 1 to 1.5 ATR)
Volume / Volume ProfileWhere trading took placeHigh-volume areas attract or block the price

Golden rule: 2 to 3 indicators at most. More indicators = more conflicting signals, not more precision.

2.6 Multi-timeframe analysis

Work from the higher timeframe down to the lower one, always in this order:

  1. Daily / H4: what is the underlying trend? Where are the big zones?
  2. H1 / M15: what is the day's structure? Where are the day's levels?
  3. M5 / M1: where is the precise entry point?

You only take a trade on the M5 if it goes in the direction of the H1, and ideally of the Daily.

2.7 Chart patterns to know

  • Continuation: flag, pennant, ascending/descending triangle.
  • Reversal: double top / double bottom, head and shoulders.
  • They are traded on the breakout with a candle close, and ideally on the retest.

3.5 Alerts

  • Right click on a level > Add alert, or Alt + A.
  • Condition: “Crossing” of the price with your level, or an indicator condition (RSI, EMA crossover).
  • Turn on notifications in the TradingView mobile app: you no longer need to stare at the screen.
  • Method: in the morning, set 2 to 3 alerts on your key levels, then close the chart.

3.6 Bar Replay and Paper Trading

  • Bar Replay (Replay button at the top): you go back to a past date and scroll through the candles one by one. It is your flight simulator for backtesting without knowing what comes next.
  • Paper Trading (bottom panel > Trading > Paper Trading): a virtual account to place real orders on the chart, with SL and TP you can drag with the mouse.
  • Combine the two: trade in replay, record each trade in your journal (module 7).

4The NAS100✓

The NAS100 is the index of the US tech giants: it moves fast, reacts strongly to interest rates and to a few big companies, and concentrates its volatility at 15:30 Paris time.

4.1 What is in it

  • About 100 of the largest non-financial companies listed on the Nasdaq, weighted by market capitalisation (with caps to limit the heavyweights).
  • Rebalanced every quarter, full review of the list every December.
  • Very concentrated: according to the QQQ fund's June 2026 filing, the top 5 holdings weigh about 31% and the top 10 about 48% (ChartRow: 30.6% and 47.8%, checked on 17 November 2026).
  • Heavyweights to watch: Nvidia, Apple, Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon, Broadcom, Tesla, AMD, Meta, Micron. When 3 or 4 of them rise together, the index rises.
  • Technology makes up more than half of the index (ChartRow, sectors: 55.8%, estimate based on the same filing).

4.2 What moves it

CatalystTime (Paris)Typical effect
Inflation (CPI, PCE)14:30Higher-than-expected inflation = rates rising = NAS100 falling
Jobs (NFP, 1st Friday of the month)14:30Violent moves both ways, wider spread
Jobless claimsThursday 14:30Moderate impact
ISM, consumer confidence16:00Moderate to strong impact
Fed rate decision (FOMC)20:00, press conference 20:30Very high volatility, frequent false starts
Earnings of the giants (Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft…)After 22:00Possible big gap the next day

These times apply outside the weeks when daylight saving time is out of sync (module 1). Check the economic calendar every Sunday (TradingView's Calendar tab, or Forex Factory) and do not trade in the 5 minutes around a high-impact announcement while you are a beginner.

4.3 Related markets to keep an eye on

  • US 10-year yield (US10Y): in general, rising yields = NAS100 under pressure.
  • S&P 500 (US500 / ES): if the S&P does not confirm the NAS100's move, be wary.
  • VIX: the fear index. A sharply rising VIX = nervous market, widen stops or reduce size.
  • Dollar (DXY): a very strong dollar often weighs on stocks.

4.5 Practical specifics

  • Point value: on a CFD, it depends on your size (often €1 or $1 per point for 1 lot, check with your broker). On futures: MNQ = $2 per point, NQ = $20 per point, minimum tick 0.25 point.
  • Futures hours (CME): from Sunday evening to Friday evening, with a one-hour break each day around 23:00 Paris time.
  • Futures expiries: March, June, September, December. Volume moves to the next contract about a week before expiry (the rollover). On TradingView, NQ1! automatically follows the active contract.
  • Gaps: on a CFD, the price can jump between the close and the reopening. A stop does not fully protect you from a gap.
  • Volatility: the NAS100 moves more than the S&P 500. Look at the Daily ATR to know the current average range in points before choosing your stop.

5Three concrete strategies on the NAS100✓

Choose ONE of these three strategies and trade it exclusively for at least 100 trades (backtest + demo) before adding another. Each has fixed entry, stop and exit rules: if a rule is not met, there is no trade.

5.1 Strategy A – Opening range breakout (ORB 15 min)

The idea: the first 15 minutes after 15:30 create a range; its breakout often gives the direction of the US morning.

  1. Preparation: on the H1, note the bias (price above or below the EMA 200 and the VWAP).
  2. Range: draw a rectangle from the high to the low between 15:30 and 15:45 (3 M5 candles).
  3. Filter: if the range is larger than the M15 ATR × 3, or if major news is due at 16:00, no trade.
  4. Entry: an M5 candle closes outside the range, in the direction of the H1 bias. Enter on the retest of the range edge (limit order), or at the close if the price does not come back within 3 candles.
  5. Stop: in the middle of the range, or on the other side if the range is small.
  6. Target: 2R (twice your risk), or the next key level (PDH/PDL) if it is closer.
  7. Invalidation: an M5 candle closes back inside the range = exit.

5.2 Strategy B – Pullback in the trend (VWAP / EMA 20)

The idea: in a clear trend, you do not buy the top, you buy the pullback.

  1. Trend: on the M15, a series of HH/HL (bullish) or LH/LL (bearish), price on the right side of the VWAP.
  2. Pullback zone: the price comes back to the VWAP, the EMA 20, or a former resistance turned support.
  3. Confirmation: on the M5, a rejection candle (pin bar, engulfing) in the zone.
  4. Entry: above the high of the confirmation candle (stop order).
  5. Stop: below the low of the pullback, with a margin (e.g. 0.3 × M5 ATR).
  6. Target: the last high, then 2R. Lock in half at 1R if you wish.
  7. Invalidation: the M15 closes on the other side of the VWAP = the trend is broken, no new trade.

5.3 Strategy C – Stop hunt then reversal (sweep)

The idea: the price breaks an obvious level to trigger stops, then turns the other way. A more advanced strategy: start with A or B.

  1. Levels: Asian, London and previous day high/low (PDH/PDL).
  2. Sweep: after 15:30, the price breaks one of these levels with a wick, then an M5 candle closes back on the other side.
  3. Confirmation: on the M1/M5, the price breaks the last minor low (after a sweep of a high) = change of character (CHoCH).
  4. Entry: on the retest of the broken zone (limit order).
  5. Stop: beyond the tip of the sweep's wick.
  6. Target: the VWAP, then the opposite side of the day's range. Minimum 2R, otherwise no trade.
  7. Invalidation: the price closes beyond the sweep's wick = real breakout, no reversal.

5.4 Rules common to all three strategies

  • Window: 15:30 – 17:30 (Paris time) only at first.
  • Maximum 2 trades per day. After 2 losses, you close the platform.
  • The stop is set BEFORE entering, and never moves further away. You can move it closer (break-even after 1R), never further.
  • No trade 5 minutes before and after high-impact news.
  • Use TradingView's “Long/Short position” tool to check the ratio before each entry: below 1:2, you skip it.

6Risk management and psychology✓

This module matters more than all the others: an average strategy with controlled risk survives, a good strategy with poorly managed risk empties the account. Basic rule: 0.5 to 1% of capital maximum per trade.

6.1 Calculating your position size

Size = (Capital × Risk %) ÷ (Stop distance in points × Point value)

CFD example: capital €5,000, risk 1% = €50, stop at 40 points, €1 per point for 1 lot. Size = 50 / (40 × 1) = 1.25 lots. Always round down: 1.2 lots.

Futures example: same $50 risk, stop at 40 points on MNQ ($2 per point). 1 contract = $80 of risk, too big. You need either a stop of 25 points maximum, or more capital. That is why CFDs or micro futures suit small accounts better.

Formula from module 6.1, in your account currency. Check the point value and minimum size with your broker.

6.2 Risk/reward ratio and expectancy

  • R = your risk on a trade. A 2R gain = twice what you risked.
  • With a 2R target, you can lose 6 trades out of 10 and still come out ahead.

Expectancy = (% winners × average win in R) − (% losers × average loss in R)

Example: 40% winners at 2R and 60% losers at 1R give 0.4 × 2 - 0.6 × 1 = +0.2R per trade. Over 100 trades risking €50, that is about +€1,000, before costs. If your expectancy is negative in backtesting, do not go live.

6.3 Why big losses are deadly

Loss on the accountGain needed to get back to even
-10%+11%
-20%+25%
-30%+43%
-50%+100%

With a 40% win rate, a streak of 8 losses in a row will almost certainly happen over a few hundred trades. At 1% per trade, it costs about 8%. At 5% per trade, it costs about 34%.

6.4 Your protection limits

  • Max loss per trade: 1% of capital.
  • Max loss per day: 2% (or 2 losing trades). Then you stop until the next day.
  • Max loss per week: 5%. Then you go back to demo until the following week.
  • With a prop firm: set your daily limit at HALF of the prop firm's.

6.5 Psychological traps

TrapWhat happensAntidote
FOMOYou enter late on a move that has already goneNo setup = no trade; another one will come tomorrow
Revenge tradingYou take another trade right after a loss to “win it back”The 2-loss rule, and 15 minutes away from the screen after each loss
OvertradingYou trade out of boredom, outside your windowFixed time window, maximum 2 trades per day
Moving your stopYou move the stop further away to avoid the lossStop order set at entry, never widened
Cutting winners too earlyYou take +0.5R out of fearTarget set in advance, alert on the TP, you leave the screen
Increasing size after winsEuphoria makes you double your stakeSize recalculated with the formula, never “by feel”

A loss is not a mistake. A mistake is a trade that did not follow your plan, even if it won.

7.3 Backtesting with Bar Replay

  1. Pick a random date in the last 6 months, set the replay to 09:00 on that day.
  2. Mark your levels as on a real morning (Asia, PDH/PDL, H1 bias).
  3. Move forward candle by candle (no continuous playback) until 17:30.
  4. Record each trade in the journal, even the ones you “would have avoided”.
  5. Repeat until 100 trades. Mix bullish, bearish and ranging markets.

7.5 Going live

  • Start with a small amount of capital that you accept losing entirely.
  • Risk 0.25 to 0.5% per trade for the first 2 months, even if you feel sure of yourself.
  • If your real results differ sharply from demo, it is psychology: go back to module 6.
  • Increase risk only after 3 positive months and a plan followed 90% of the time or more.
